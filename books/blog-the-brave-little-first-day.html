I Made a Children's Picture Book With AI Illustrations — Here's What I Learned About SEL, Storytelling, and Technology

Published August 1, 2026 by Wilson Yeu

Every child knows the feeling

Starting something new is terrifying — especially when you're small.

The butterflies in your tummy. The lump in your throat. The overwhelming urge to just... stay home.

I wanted to write a book about that feeling. Not to fix it — children don't need fixing — but to name it. To say: this feeling has a name, and you are not alone in it.

That book became The Brave Little First Day — a 32-page picture book for ages 3-8 about a girl named Maya, her seashell, and the courage it takes to walk through a new door.

The story in 30 seconds

Maya wakes up with butterflies dancing in her tummy. It's her first day at a new school. She packs her backpack with a pencil, an eraser, and one small seashell from the beach — for courage.

At lunch, she sits alone. The butterflies turn into a heavy rock.

Then a boy with glasses and a gap-tooth smile asks about her seashell. He has one too — a green stone from his grandma's garden.

And just like that, the butterflies start flying together.

Why SEL matters in picture books

Social-Emotional Learning (SEL) isn't a buzzword — it's the foundation of how children learn to be human.

Research shows that children who develop emotional vocabulary early:

  • Navigate transitions more smoothly
  • Build stronger peer relationships
  • Develop resilience that lasts a lifetime

But here's the thing: kids don't learn SEL from lectures. They learn it from stories. From characters they can see themselves in. From feelings that are named, not hidden.

That's what The Brave Little First Day does. It gives children:

  • A vocabulary — butterflies aren't scary, they're excitement waiting to land
  • A strategy — find courage in small things (a seashell, a stone, a breath)
  • A truth — nervousness and excitement feel the same, and that's okay

The AI illustration journey

Here's where it gets interesting (and where I learned the most).

I'm not a professional illustrator. I'm a parent and a storyteller. When I started this project, I had two choices:

  1. Wait years to find and afford an illustrator
  2. Use AI tools to bring the story to life now

I chose option 2. And the results surprised me.

What worked beautifully

  • Consistent character design — AI image generation has gotten remarkably good at maintaining a character's look across multiple scenes.
  • Warm, inviting color palettes — The Pixar-inspired style creates illustrations that feel like a hug.
  • Speed of iteration — I could test different emotional beats visually.

What I struggled with

  • Specific details — Getting a seashell to look just right in a child's hand took more tries than I want to admit.
  • Emotional subtlety — AI is great at big emotions. Nuanced micro-expressions are still tricky.
  • The uncanny gap — There's a narrow sweet spot between "obviously AI" and "almost real but something's off."

What I'd tell anyone starting this journey

  1. Start with the story, not the tool. The illustrations serve the narrative, not the other way around.
  2. Iterate relentlessly. Your first 50 attempts are practice. The magic starts at attempt 51.
  3. Test with real children. My daughter's reaction to Maya's seashell scene was worth every hour of work.
  4. Be transparent. I chose to clearly disclose that illustrations are AI-assisted. Parents deserve to know.

What's inside the book

  • An activity page — Draw your own brave moment with reflection prompts
  • A parent/teacher guide — Discussion questions to deepen the conversation after reading
